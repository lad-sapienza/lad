## What’s new: AITracer is no longer experimental

> **Update, October 2026 — version 0.4.0.** This article, first published in April 2026 to introduce the plugin, has been updated to follow its development. The news comes first; the rest of the text describes the plugin as it is today.

A lot has happened since the first presentation:

- **No longer an experimental plugin.** Since version 0.2.0 the *experimental* flag has been removed and the plugin is a stable release. It is **available in the official QGIS plugin repository**: just search for it in *Plugins → Manage and Install Plugins*, with no need to enable experimental plugins any more. Community interest has grown too: at the time of writing the plugin has passed **1,500 downloads** from the official repository, against 6 at the time of the first presentation.
- **Field-tested.** AITracer was used extensively during the **2026 excavation season of the [Sapienza Archaeological Mission at Çuka e Ajtoit](/en/ricerca/missione-archeologica-sapienza-a-cuka-e-ajtoit-albania/)** and, back at the lab, for documentation work. Daily use on real data and under tight schedules directly shaped the changes listed below. The time saved cannot be quantified in absolute terms, but the effect was clear: AITracer **changed the scale of the documentation we collected**, making it practical to digitize in detail what would previously have been recorded far more summarily.
- **Topological snapping to neighbouring polygons (new in 0.4.0).** Tick **Snap to neighbours** and the new polygon shares its boundary with the polygons already in the output layer: overlaps are cut, small gaps are closed, and the outline follows one coherent perimeter even across several neighbours, like QGIS’s tracing tool. Matching vertices are added to the neighbours too, without changing their shape, so the layer stays topologically valid. It is meant for digitizing adjacent features, such as the stones of a wall, and opens the way to analyses that need correct topology. A single undo (QGIS’s Ctrl+Z) reverts everything. More details [below](#topological-snapping-to-neighbouring-polygons).
- **Choice of model.** From the panel you can pick SAM2.1 **Tiny** (default, fastest), **Base+** (cleaner boundaries at little extra cost) or **Large** (most accurate, for hard cases). Larger models are downloaded, after confirmation, only the first time you select them; downloads are streamed to disk with real progress and a *Cancel* button. Your choice is remembered between sessions.
- **Simplification in screen pixels.** The Douglas-Peucker slider now works in pixels (0 to 6, default 2.8), with finer steps and a live vertex count. Its effect is the same at any zoom level and in any coordinate reference system, and the value is remembered.
- **Edit the last polygon.** One click on **✎ Edit last polygon** (or the **E** key) selects the polygon you just accepted and switches to QGIS’s Vertex tool so you can fix it.
- **Pause and resume.** If you pick another QGIS tool (Vertex, Identify, Pan…), AITracer pauses instead of getting out of sync; **▶ Resume** picks up with the same model, output layer and field values.
- **Preset field values.** When you choose an existing output layer, a panel lets you preset attributes (using the editor widgets configured in the layer) that are applied to every accepted polygon — handy for digitizing series of features that share attributes, such as the same stratigraphic unit or material.
- **More stable masks.** Refinement reuses the previous mask as its starting point, so successive clicks change the result far less.
- **Model weights stay safe.** Model files are kept in `~/.aitracer/weights/` and survive plugin reinstalls.
- **Compatibility and code quality.** Qt6 and Windows installation issues fixed, and the plugin passes the official QGIS repository quality checks.

## The original presentation

The rest of the article is the original presentation text, updated where needed.

We are pleased to present **AITracer by LAD**, a plugin for **QGIS 4** that brings artificial intelligence directly into the raster digitization workflow in archaeology. Developed by the Digital Archaeology Laboratory (LAD) at Sapienza, AITracer lets you vectorize any feature visible on a georeferenced raster layer — aerial photographs, drone orthophotos, scanned plans, photogrammetric surveys — with a simple mouse click.

The plugin is released under the **GNU GPL 3 license** and is available in the official QGIS plugin repository and on GitHub:  
🔗 [https://github.com/lad-sapienza/ai-tracer](https://github.com/lad-sapienza/ai-tracer)

## The problem: manual digitization is slow

Anyone who works daily with raster data in archaeology knows how much time manual vectorization takes: tracing by hand the outlines of wall structures, postholes, stratigraphic cuts or areas of ceramic fragments on a high-resolution orthophoto is a precise but extremely laborious operation. The results depend heavily on the operator's experience and concentration, and the time required multiplies quickly when there are dozens or hundreds of geometries to digitize.

AITracer does not remove the archaeologist's judgment — who always remains the interpreter — but radically speeds up the mechanical tracing phase, leaving the operator in full control of the result.

---

## How it works: SAM2 in the service of archaeology

AITracer is based on **SAM2** (*Segment Anything Model 2*), the image segmentation model developed by Meta FAIR and released as open source. SAM2 can identify and outline objects within an image starting from simple point-based cues — so-called *prompts* — without having been specifically trained on archaeological images.

The workflow is as follows:

1. Load a georeferenced raster layer in QGIS and zoom to the area of interest.
2. Activate the plugin via the **AITracer by LAD** panel and click on the object to digitize.
3. SAM2 analyzes the visible portion of the canvas and returns the outline of the feature within moments, displayed as a green preview polygon directly on the map.
4. Accept the polygon (**Enter** key) or refine the result with additional clicks before accepting it.

The model runs locally, on your own computer, without sending any data to external services.

## Positive and negative prompts

The heart of the system is iterative interaction with the model through *prompts*:

- **Left click** — *positive prompt*: tells the model a point that must be included in the polygon.
- **Right click** — *negative prompt*: indicates a point to exclude, useful for refining the outline when the model includes unwanted areas.

Each additional click refines the segmentation without recomputing the entire image analysis, thanks to an image-encoding cache that the backend keeps for the whole duration of the session.

## Real-time geometric simplification

The masks produced by SAM2 can contain a very high number of vertices. AITracer includes a **Douglas-Peucker simplification** slider that lets you control the vertex density of the final polygon before accepting it, expressed in screen pixels, with a range from 0 (no simplification) to 6 px and a default of 2.8 px. The vertex count updates in real time, and because the unit is pixels the effect is the same at any zoom level and in any coordinate reference system.

## Topological snapping to neighbouring polygons

When you digitize features that touch each other — the stones of a wall, the units of a paving, the fragments of a floor — the outlines of adjacent polygons should coincide. Traced one at a time, they leave gaps and overlaps instead. With the **Snap to neighbours** option in the panel, AITracer attaches the new polygon to those already in the output layer:

1. parts of the new polygon covering an existing polygon are cut away;
2. the outlines of all nearby polygons are treated as one network, and every edge of the new polygon that runs along it is replaced by the path on the network, crossing from one neighbour to the next where their outlines meet; where neighbours do not touch, the outline joins the two nearest points directly;
3. vertices that now lie on a neighbour’s boundary are added to that neighbour, so the two outlines have coincident vertices. The neighbours’ shapes do not change.

The snap distance is set next to the checkbox, in screen pixels (default 8 px), so it behaves the same at any zoom level. The preview already shows the snapped outline, and a single undo in QGIS reverts the new polygon together with the vertices added to its neighbours. The setting is remembered between sessions.

Some practical notes:

- Snapping works only if the output layer uses the same coordinate reference system as the map.
- A lone vertex near another polygon is not snapped: the minimum contact is one segment.
- Polygons covered for more than half by the new one, or lying entirely inside it, are left alone: that usually means a different object is being traced.
- Snapped polygons can end up with more vertices, because they inherit those of the outlines they follow. The simplification slider and QGIS’s own tools help reduce them if needed.

## Undo the last point

If a click was placed in the wrong spot, there is no need to cancel the entire session. Ctrl+Z removes the last point entered — positive or negative — and immediately recalculates the segmentation with the remaining points. You can go back as far as you like, all the way to clearing every prompt.

## Accepting and saving

Once satisfied with the outline, press **Enter** (or the *Accept* button in the panel). The polygon is automatically added to a temporary in-memory layer called **AITracer**, created by the plugin with a semi-transparent orange style. The layer is fully integrated with the QGIS project and can be exported to any vector format.

## Choosing the destination layer

By default, accepted polygons are inserted into a temporary in-memory layer called AITracer. However, the panel lets you choose any polygon vector layer already present in the project as the destination, via a drop-down menu that updates automatically. On the first acceptance, the menu is set to the newly created AITracer layer, so subsequent clicks flow into it without having to select anything.

## First installation: everything automatic

The plugin manages its own computational infrastructure autonomously. On first launch it:

1. creates a Python virtual environment in `~/.aitracer/venv`;
2. installs the necessary dependencies (FastAPI, PyTorch, SAM2, OpenCV);
3. downloads the SAM2-tiny model weights (~156 MB).

A progress dialog keeps the user informed at every stage. Subsequent activations take only a few seconds.

## Installing the plugin

AITracer is available on the **official QGIS plugin repository**: [plugins.qgis.org/plugins/aitracer](https://plugins.qgis.org/plugins/aitracer/). This makes installing and updating the plugin extremely simple, with two methods available.

### Method 1 (recommended): directly from QGIS

1. In QGIS, open **Plugins → Manage and Install Plugins**.
2. On the **All** tab, search for *AITracer* and click **Install Plugin**. (Since version 0.2.0 the plugin is no longer experimental, so there is no need to enable experimental plugins in Settings.)

With this method, QGIS will automatically notify you when new versions are available.

### Method 2: manual download from the repository

Alternatively, you can download the ZIP package directly from [plugins.qgis.org/plugins/aitracer](https://plugins.qgis.org/plugins/aitracer/) via a web browser and install it in QGIS with **Plugins → Manage and Install Plugins → Install from ZIP**.

### Method 3: from GitHub releases

For those who want to test specific or pre-release versions, the `aitracer-vX.Y.Z.zip` packages are also always available from the [GitHub Releases page](https://github.com/lad-sapienza/ai-tracer/releases).

QGIS 4.0 is required. The necessary Python runtime is automatically downloaded and installed by the plugin on first launch via python-build-standalone: no manual Python installation is required.

## Important usage notes: AITracer does not read raster files, it captures the screen

AITracer uses the screen, not the raster file: here is why this matters

AITracer captures a screenshot of the QGIS canvas at the moment you click. It does not read the original raster data. This is a deliberate design choice: it keeps memory consumption low and segmentation fast even on modest hardware. This way, no multi-gigabyte file needs to be decoded or loaded into RAM.

This choice has three practical consequences that are important to know before you start digitizing:

- **Resolution depends on the zoom level**. If you are zoomed far out, each screen pixel represents a large geographic area and the polygon edges will be approximate. Zooming in until the feature fills most of the canvas gives the most precise result.
- **The feature must be fully visible**. Any part of an object that extends beyond the edge of the *canvas* is simply absent from the screenshot. If a wall, a plot boundary or any other shape runs off the screen, only the visible portion will be included in the polygon. Before clicking, pan and zoom the view so that the entire feature is visible.
- **The canvas locks during the session**: this is intentional. After the first click, the extent of the *canvas* is frozen until acceptance or cancellation. Panning or zooming the view during the session would invalidate the correspondence between pixel coordinates and geographic coordinates, producing incorrect geometries. The lock is released the moment you press Enter or Esc.

*Practical tip: before activating the tool, zoom in on the feature to digitize leaving a small margin around it, and make sure the raster of interest is the only visible layer. Then activate, click, refine and accept.*

## A note on method: pair programming with AI

This plugin was also born as a methodological experiment. The entire design and development process — from the system architecture to individual Python functions, from backend management to the panel UI — was carried out in close collaboration with **[Claude](https://claude.ai)** by Anthropic, an assistant based on large language models.

The result is code that works and has been field-tested at the Çuka e Ajtoit excavation and in the lab work that followed, but above all a concrete reflection on how generative AI can support — not replace — the researcher in producing digital tools for humanities research. Design control, interpretive choices and verification of results always remained with the human researcher.
